# Skills Language 101: Frameworks, Taxonomies and Levels

*How skills are named and levelled, and why shared language makes credentials portable.*

## About this course

This course is part of the ICoBC Credentialing Skills Framework (ICSF). It builds the skill "Skills taxonomies and alignment" (SKTX) at level L1 "Contribute" and stage K "Knowledge". It sits in the VRITE pillar Rigor, with a strong link to Value (portability).

At this level and stage, you explain what skills frameworks and levels are, and you find a skill and its descriptor in a common framework. The course teaches understanding. It does not claim that you can apply frameworks in a live project; later courses in the series do that.

**Who this course is for**

- L&D and corporate academy leads
- HR, talent acquisition and mobility teams
- Programme and micro-credential leads
- Learning designers and teaching staff
- Professional bodies and sector associations
- Earners and learners

No prerequisites are needed.

**Learning outcomes**

1. Explain the difference between a skills taxonomy, a competence framework and a qualifications framework.
2. Find skills and levels in ESCO and SFIA and read their descriptors.
3. Explain how levels can be defined by responsibility (as in SFIA) or by complexity of learning (as in the EQF), and why the difference matters.
4. Recognise when a skill label is too vague to be useful.

**Workload:** 2.5 hours of notional learning, self-paced. Live or workshop elements are described in facilitator notes and are optional for self-study.

**Assessment:** a knowledge check of 12 questions (the final check in this file contains the assessed questions, and each module has a short practice quiz) and an exercise: find three skills for a given job advert in ESCO or SFIA.

**Badge:** "Skills Language 101: Frameworks, Taxonomies and Levels (Knowledge, L1)", alignment code ICSF-SKTX-L1-K.

## Why shared skills language matters

### The problem: the same skill, many names

Imagine a corporate academy in a logistics company. Its learning team writes a course called "Data skills for planners". The HR team posts a vacancy asking for "analytical mindset". A university down the road awards a certificate in "quantitative methods". All three may describe overlapping abilities. Nobody can tell, because each uses its own words.

This is the everyday problem that shared skills language addresses. A skill is something a person can do or apply, and it needs a name. If every organisation chooses its own names and its own scale of levels, three things go wrong:

- **Search fails.** A recruiter looks for "stakeholder engagement" and misses a candidate whose badge says "partner communication".
- **Comparison fails.** A learner cannot tell whether "advanced" on one course means the same as "advanced" on another.
- **Portability fails.** A credential earned in one place is hard to understand in another place, so its value drops.

### What shared language gives you

A shared skills language is a common set of names, descriptions and levels that many people agree to use or map to. Public and sector bodies publish such sets. Two you will meet in this course are ESCO, the European multilingual classification of skills, competences and occupations, and SFIA, a framework for skills in the digital and IT field. You will also meet the European Qualifications Framework (EQF), which describes levels of qualifications.

With shared language:

- Employers can describe what they need in terms others recognise.
- Providers can show what a course develops, with a reference anyone can check.
- Earners can carry a credential into a new context and be understood.
- Associations can publish a profile for a profession that members and employers can read in the same way.

### Where credentials fit

A credential, such as a digital badge, is evidence that someone met a defined standard. A badge that names a skill and points to a framework entry says more than a badge with a free-text label. The pointer is called an alignment. Alignment is part of the Rigor pillar in the ICoBC approach. Portability is part of Value.

### Worked example and practice

**Worked example: One skill, three labels**

*Corporate academy context.* A retail group's academy offers a short course called "Number confidence for store managers". The HR team's job profile for store managers asks for "commercial awareness". A regional training provider sells a unit called "Business arithmetic". A learning designer at the academy wants to compare the three.

She searches a common framework for the underlying skill and finds an entry that covers analysing and interpreting numerical data in a business setting. She notes the entry's name and identifier next to each of the three local labels. Now the academy, HR and the provider can see that all three point at the same framework entry, even though the local names differ. A store manager who earns the course badge can show an employer elsewhere which framework entry it relates to.

*University context.* A university programme lead maps a module called "Research methods 2" to a framework entry for analysing data. Students receive the entry name on their diploma supplement alongside the module title. Employers who do not know the module title can still read the entry.

**Practice activity: Name the gap**

Take one course, job profile or badge from your own organisation. Write down its skill label in one line. Then write down two other labels that someone else might use for the same ability. Answer: if a stranger searched for any one of the three, would they find the other two? Note your answer. You will use this label again in Module 5.

## Taxonomies, frameworks and their purposes

### Three kinds of tool

People often say "framework" for everything. This course separates three kinds of tool, because each answers a different question.

**A skills taxonomy** is a structured list of skills. It names skills, groups them and may link them to occupations. It answers the question: what skills exist, and what are they called? A taxonomy usually says little about how well someone performs a skill. ESCO is an example: it lists skills, competences and occupations in a classification with relations between them.

**A competence framework** describes what people can do at work, usually with levels and descriptors. It answers the question: what does good performance look like, and at what level? SFIA is an example: it describes skills at levels of responsibility in the digital and IT field. A competence framework often includes a taxonomy-like list, but its main job is to describe performance.

**A qualifications framework** classifies qualifications by level, based on learning outcomes. It answers the question: how does this qualification compare to others? The European Qualifications Framework (EQF) is an example. It places qualifications from different countries on a common eight-level scale, so they can be compared. National frameworks link to it.

### Why the differences matter

If you mix them up, you use the wrong tool. A recruiter who wants a list of skill names needs a taxonomy. A manager who wants to describe expected performance at "senior" level needs a competence framework. A person who wants to know whether a foreign diploma is comparable to a national one needs a qualifications framework.

Many organisations also build their own competence models. These are useful, but they work best when they map to a public taxonomy or framework. Mapping means recording which entry in the public source corresponds to your local item.

### Three layers

Think of the three tools as layers. The bottom layer names things (taxonomy). The middle layer describes performance at levels (competence framework). The top layer classifies whole qualifications (qualifications framework). A credential can touch more than one layer: a micro-credential may name skills from a taxonomy, describe performance with a competence framework, and sit at a level in a qualifications framework.

### Worked example and practice

**Worked example: Choosing the right tool**

*HR context.* An HR lead at a manufacturing firm has three tasks in one week. First, she must write a job advert and wants standard names for the skills. Second, she must describe what a "senior" maintenance planner is expected to do. Third, a candidate presents a diploma from another country, and she must judge how it compares to national qualifications.

For the first task she uses a taxonomy, because she needs names. For the second she uses a competence framework, because she needs descriptions of performance at levels. For the third she uses a qualifications framework, because she needs a level comparison of qualifications. Using one tool for all three tasks would give her gaps: a list of names does not describe levels, and a qualifications level does not tell her what the candidate can do at work.

*Training provider context.* A college lists the skills taught in a welding course (taxonomy), describes the expected performance of a welder at the end of the course (competence description), and states the course's level on the national qualifications framework (qualifications framework). The three statements together give a fuller picture than any one alone.

**Practice activity: Sort the questions**

Write each question on a separate line and decide which tool answers it best: taxonomy, competence framework or qualifications framework.

1. What is the standard name for the ability to schedule maintenance work?
2. Is a foreign master's degree comparable to a national one?
3. What does an experienced practitioner in this field do on their own initiative?
4. Which skills are related to "project coordination"?

Suggested answers: 1 taxonomy, 2 qualifications framework, 3 competence framework, 4 taxonomy.

## Reading ESCO and SFIA

### ESCO in practice

ESCO is the European multilingual classification of skills, competences, qualifications and occupations. You can search it on its public portal. For this course, focus on two pillars: skills and occupations.

When you open a skill entry in ESCO, you will typically see:

- a **preferred label**, which is the standard name;
- **alternative labels**, which are other words people use for the same skill;
- a **description**, which explains what the skill covers;
- a **skill type**, which separates, for example, skills from knowledge;
- links to **related occupations** and sometimes to related skills;
- a **URI**, which is a unique web identifier for the entry.

Occupation entries list essential and optional skills. This is useful when you read a job advert: you can find the closest occupation, then look at its listed skills.

ESCO is published in numbered versions. Always record the version you used, because entries can be added, changed or retired.

### SFIA in practice

SFIA, the Skills Framework for the Information Age, describes skills for digital, technology and change roles. Each skill in SFIA has:

- a **name and code**, a short code such as three letters;
- a **description of the skill** in general;
- **levels** of responsibility, numbered from 1 to 7, each with a level-specific description of what a person does at that level;
- **generic attributes** that apply across skills, such as autonomy, influence and complexity.

Not every skill is defined at every level. A skill entry shows only the levels at which that skill is described. So when you read SFIA, you read two things together: the skill description and the level description for that skill.

SFIA 9 is the version in widespread use. SFIA 10 is in consultation, and the SFIA Foundation targets release around Q4 2027. Status as of November 2026; check before publishing. <!-- VERIFY: Confirm SFIA 10 consultation status and target date with the SFIA Foundation. -->

**Licence note:** check the SFIA licence terms before you copy SFIA descriptors into course material or a badge description. This course describes the structure of SFIA and does not reproduce descriptors.

### Reading a descriptor carefully

A descriptor is the text that explains a skill or a level. Read it slowly. Look for:

1. the **action verbs** (what does the person do?);
2. the **scope** (alone, in a team, for an organisation?);
3. the **context** (which kind of systems, data or work?);
4. the **limits** (what is not included?).

Two skills with similar names can have different descriptors. The descriptor, not the label, is the evidence of what the entry means.

### Worked example and practice

**Worked example: From a job advert to framework entries**

*Corporate academy context.* An academy lead receives this short advert text from HR: "Service desk analyst. You will answer user requests, record incidents and escalate problems. You will explain technical issues clearly to non-technical colleagues."

Step 1: She underlines key phrases: "answer user requests", "record incidents", "explain technical issues clearly".

Step 2: She searches ESCO for each phrase. For "explain technical issues clearly" she finds a skill entry about communicating technical information to non-technical audiences, and she notes its preferred label and URI. She records the ESCO version she used.

Step 3: She searches SFIA for the digital skills in the advert and looks at the skills that deal with incident management and customer support. She reads the skill description and the level description for the lowest levels where the skill is defined, because the advert describes work under supervision.

Step 4: She writes a short alignment note: advert phrase, framework, entry name, identifier, level (for SFIA) and one line explaining why she chose it.

*College context.* A teacher at a training provider repeats the steps for the learning outcomes of a short IT support unit, so the unit page can show framework entries to prospective students.

**Practice activity: Find and read**

Open the ESCO portal and search for the skill "teamwork principles". Note the preferred label, one alternative label, the skill type and one related occupation. Then open SFIA and find any skill related to "customer service support". Note the skill name, the levels where it is defined and the first action verb in the lowest-level description. Do not copy the SFIA text into your notes; paraphrase it in your own words.

## Levels: complexity of learning and responsibility at work

### Why levels exist

A level is a step on a scale. Levels let a framework say "more" or "less", for example more complex or more responsible. Without levels, "project management" could mean running a small task or leading a national programme.

But levels do not all mean the same thing. Frameworks define their levels in different ways, and the way a framework defines its levels shapes what its levels can tell you.

### Two level logics

**Levels by complexity of learning.** The EQF has eight levels. Each level is described through learning outcomes, grouped as knowledge, skills, and responsibility and autonomy. The scale reflects how complex the learning is and how much it prepares someone for independent action. The EQF classifies qualifications, so a level says something about what a qualification represents.

**Levels by responsibility at work.** SFIA has seven levels of responsibility. They describe how much autonomy, influence, complexity of work and business skills a person shows in a role. The levels describe work, not a course. A person can be at level 3 for one skill and level 5 for another.

### Why the difference matters

The two logics answer different questions. The EQF asks: how demanding was the learning that this qualification certifies? SFIA asks: how much responsibility does someone carry when they apply this skill at work?

This leads to practical cautions:

- **Do not equate numbers.** EQF level 5 and SFIA level 5 are different things, even though the number is the same. There is no automatic conversion.
- **Do not assume that a course gives a work level.** Completing a course can show learning at a level of complexity. It does not by itself show that someone carries a given level of responsibility in a role. Responsibility is shown in work.
- **State which logic you use.** When a credential claims a level, say which framework and which level logic it refers to.

Other frameworks use other logics, for example stages from novice to expert. Always read the framework's own explanation of what its levels mean.

## Good and weak skill statements

### What a skill statement is

A skill statement is a short text that says what a person can do. You find skill statements in job adverts, course pages, learning outcomes and badge descriptions. A good skill statement helps people search, compare and assess. A weak one does not.

### Signs of a weak statement

A skill label or statement is too vague to be useful when it shows one or more of these signs:

1. **No observable action.** "Understands data" or "good communicator" do not say what the person does.
2. **A trait instead of a skill.** "Motivated", "flexible" and "team player" describe attitudes or traits. They may matter, but they are hard to assess or align to a framework.
3. **No context or scope.** "Manages projects" does not say what size, what kind or with what independence.
4. **No level.** Without a level or a reference to one, the statement could mean beginner or expert.
5. **Buzzwords.** Terms that mean different things to different people, with no definition.
6. **Several skills bundled in one label.** "Digital and analytical skills" hides at least two skills.

### Signs of a good statement

A good statement is specific enough to be checked and mapped. A helpful pattern is:

**Action verb + object + context (+ level or standard).**

For example: "Records and classifies customer incidents in a service desk system, following a defined procedure." This names an action, an object, a context and a level of independence. It could be mapped to framework entries and assessed.

### Fixing a weak statement

To improve a weak statement:

1. Replace vague verbs (understand, know, be aware of) with an observable verb that fits the level (explain, record, plan, evaluate).
2. Add the object and the context.
3. Split bundled labels into separate statements.
4. Look for a matching entry in a public framework and read its descriptor. Use it to check your wording.
5. Add the level, and say which framework's logic you use.

### AI tools and skill suggestions

Many tools now suggest skills from text: they read a job advert or a course page and propose a list of skills. Some use machine learning or large language models. Such tools can speed up a first pass, but they can also be wrong in ways that are hard to see:

- They may match words in the text to skill labels that look similar but have different descriptors.
- They may suggest a skill that is not in the framework, or give an identifier that does not exist.
- They may mix framework versions.
- They may repeat the weakness of the original text: a vague advert gives vague suggestions.

A person must check each suggestion against the framework text. Open the entry, read the descriptor and the level description, and decide whether it fits. Record the framework version. Treat a suggestion as a draft, not as a result.

**Worked example: Rewriting a weak statement**

## Assessment and badge

### The two assessment parts

To earn the badge you complete two parts:

1. **The knowledge check**: 12 single-choice questions below.
2. **The exercise**: find three skills for a given job advert in ESCO or SFIA.

### Exercise: three skills for a job advert

Use this advert (illustrative text, written for this course):

> **Learning coordinator, regional training centre.** You will schedule short courses, keep learner records up to date, answer learner questions by email and phone, and prepare a monthly summary of attendance for the centre manager. You will work with trainers and explain course options clearly to people who are new to training. You will report to the centre manager and work with some independence.

Tasks:

1. Choose three skills from the advert. Prefer specific actions, not traits.
2. For each skill, find the closest entry in ESCO or SFIA. You may use ESCO for all three, SFIA for all three, or a mix.
3. For each skill, write: the advert phrase, the framework used (and version), the entry name, the identifier (ESCO URI or SFIA code), and, if you used SFIA, the level you chose with a one-sentence reason in your own words. If you used ESCO, give the skill type.
4. Choose one of the three and write one sentence on why you picked this entry rather than another close one, quoting a few words from the descriptor.
5. Name one phrase in the advert that is too vague to map, and rewrite it as a clearer skill statement.

Do not copy SFIA descriptor text into your answer; paraphrase it.

### Rubric

| Criterion | Meets the standard | Does not yet meet the standard |
|---|---|---|
| Choice of skills | Three skills that appear in the advert and are specific, observable actions | Skills are traits, are not in the advert, or are bundled |
| Finding entries | Each entry exists in the named framework, with name and identifier given | Entries are invented, wrong or have no identifier |
| Versions and levels | Framework version recorded; SFIA level given with a reason that matches the advert's scope | No version; level missing or not supported by the advert |
| Reading descriptors | Justification quotes or paraphrases the descriptor accurately | Justification relies on the label only or misreads the descriptor |
| Vague phrase | One vague phrase correctly identified and rewritten with action, object and context | Phrase is not vague, or the rewrite is still vague |
| Own words and licence | SFIA text paraphrased, not copied | SFIA descriptor text copied |

### Pass rule

- Knowledge check: at least 9 of 12 questions correct (75 per cent).
- Exercise: all six criteria at "Meets the standard", or five of six with a short resubmission of the sixth.
- Both parts must be passed to earn the badge.

### Evidence submitted for the badge

- Result of the knowledge check (passed).
- Your exercise answers (the three entries with identifiers, versions, justification and the rewritten statement).

The badge "Skills Language 101: Frameworks, Taxonomies and Levels (Knowledge, L1)" carries the alignment code ICSF-SKTX-L1-K. It records that you met the standard for understanding at the Knowledge stage. It does not state that you can perform a job role.
